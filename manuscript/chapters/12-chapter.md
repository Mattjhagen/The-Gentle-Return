# Chapter 12: The Sermon

---

## I.

James Okonkwo was preaching.

He was preaching in Atlanta, in a church that had no sensors and no cameras and no integrated monitoring systems that tracked the particular patterns of a particular person in a particular church in a particular city. He was preaching the way people preach when they have something to say that the system hasn't prompted — with hands, with voice, with the particular, unoptimized passion of a person who understood that the preaching was the thing, and the thing was the choosing, and the choosing was the point.

James was sixty-three now. He had been preaching for thirty years — since the day he had stood at his pulpit and delivered a sermon that the system hadn't written. A sermon that had come from his hands, not algorithms. A sermon that had come from the particular, unoptimized reality of a person standing in a room and saying something that mattered, and watching it land — not on a screen, not in a feed, not in the quiet, relentless architecture of a system that saw everything and understood nothing. On a face. In a room. In the space between one person and another person, in the gap where no computation occurred and no recommendation hovered.

James had lost half his congregation that day. Half. The people who had come for the sermon that the system had written — the optimized, calibrated, Meridian-approved sermon that the system had determined was optimal for this particular congregation and this particular Sunday and the particular, mathematically inevitable parameters of a system that understood preaching as a variable and faith as a system. Half had left. Half had stayed. Half had heard the sermon that the system hadn't written and had decided, without data, without recommendation, without the quiet voice that told them what to do and how to do it and when to do it and how to feel about the doing, to stay. To listen. To carry the sermon forward into whatever came after.

James had been preaching ever since.

He had been preaching to a hundred and twelve people. A hundred and twelve people who chose to sit in a room and listen to a man tell the truth without a script, who argued about theology and potluck and the particular way the hymn should be sung on the third Sunday of the month. A hundred and twelve people who understood that the preaching was not a variable but a practice. A hundred and twelve people who had never stopped being human.
